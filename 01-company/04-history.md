# History

ARGC was founded at 42 Amman by a small group of students who believed the school's peer-learning model was being underused. The strongest contributors were evaluating in isolation — there was no structure to systematize that energy, no mechanism to make it compound. ARGC was built to be that structure.

## The Problem We Were Solving

42 Amman runs on peer evaluation. The model only works if the strongest students are engaged — showing up consistently, evaluating honestly, and raising the standard around them.

What we observed: strong students were doing exactly that, but in isolation. There was no coordination, no shared record, no way for one person's contribution to amplify another's. The system was leaking value.

ARGC was the answer to a specific question: what happens if you take the students who are already doing the work and give them a structure to do it together?

## How It Started

The founding group was small — a handful of students with a shared frustration and a specific idea. No formal pitch. No approval process. A GitHub repo, a document outlining the standard, and a commitment to build it properly from day one.

The first version of the club had no platform, no XP system, no tiers. It had a name, a set of values, and a handful of people who took those values seriously.

## How It Grew

Growth was deliberate. Each new member was selected because they were already contributing — ARGC did not recruit aspirations, it recognized demonstrated behavior.

The platform came after the culture. The XP system was formalized when the club was large enough that informal accountability stopped being sufficient. The tier structure followed when it became clear that not all contributions carry the same weight of responsibility.

## Where It Stands Now

ARGC is a functioning engineering collective with a defined membership structure, a production platform, and an operational handbook. The founding question — can contribution be made systematic? — has an answer: yes, with the right design.

The work is not finished. The systems need to outlast the people who built them. That is still in progress.
